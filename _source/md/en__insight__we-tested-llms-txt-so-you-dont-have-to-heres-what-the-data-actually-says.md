# We Tested LLMs.txt So You Don’t Have To. Here’s What the Data Actually Says.

_Everyone in the SEO and GEO space has an opinion on LLMs.txt. At GO MO Group, we decided to let the data do the talking._ Because AI search evolves so quickly, we view every experiment as a snapshot in time, not a permanent verdict. As platforms, standards, and user behavior change, we continuously revisit our assumptions and validate them against new data.

![](https://www.gomogroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/llms-txt-featured-image.webp)

When the buzz around LLMs.txt started building at the tail end of 2024, our instinct wasn't to immediately roll it out across our client portfolio. It was to ask the same question we always ask: **does this actually move the needle?**

So we ran the experiment. Four clients. Different industries. Different audiences. Different markets. And six months of tracking. Here's what our data showed at this point in time, and why we'll continue monitoring this as AI search evolves.

## **First, Let's Be Clear About What LLMs.txt Actually Is**

The easiest way to misunderstand LLMs.txt is to call it "robots.txt for AI" — a description that has spread like wildfire and is categorically **wrong**.

Robots.txt controls how bots crawl your site. LLMs.txt does something different: it's a markdown-formatted file sitting at your domain root that presents your core content in a clean, ad-free, navigation-free format — theoretically making it easier for large language models to consume and understand what your site is really about.

Only 10% of websites have one, and based on what we found, that may not be a problem. [Click here](https://searchengineland.com/llms.txt) to see what a basic LLMs.txt file actually looks like in practice. Clean, structured, no clutter. The idea is sound.

The problem is what happens next: nobody reads it.

![](data:image/svg+xml,%3Csvg%20xmlns='http://www.w3.org/2000/svg'%20viewBox='0%200%20915%20366'%3E%3C/svg%3E)

In theory, it's structured and straightforward. It sounds like the perfect shortcut for GEO. In practice, it runs into one fundamental problem: **the major AI platforms haven't agreed to use it.**

Not Anthropic. Not OpenAI. Not Google. None of them have announced support for the proposed standard. And as Google's John Mueller put it bluntly in a recent [Reddit discussion:](https://www.reddit.com/r/SEO/comments/1mibb1m/comment/n76qsjm/)

_"As far as I know, none of the AI services have said they're using LLMs.TXT (and you can tell when you look at your server logs that they don't even check for it). To me, it's comparable to the keywords meta tag – this is what a site-owner claims their site is about… (Is the site really like that? well, you can check it. At that point, why not just check the site directly?)"_

Mueller's keywords meta tag analogy is sharper than it sounds. That tag died because search engines stopped trusting site owners to self-describe accurately. It became a spam vector overnight. LLMs.txt faces the exact same structural problem: what stops anyone from showing AI bots one set of content while serving users something entirely different? Nothing. And that's a problem no major platform wants on their hands.

## **The Experiment: Real Clients, Real Data**

We implemented LLMs.txt files across clients in different industries (B2B technology and B2C visual commerce) spanning different audience sizes, markets, and AI visibility starting points. We then tracked performance across ChatGPT, Google AI Overviews, AI Mode, and Gemini for up to three months post-implementation.

Across both, the pattern was the same.

AI Visibility Scores were essentially unmoved. One client held flat at 14 across the first two months before edging to 15 in month three. A single point of movement over a quarter that sits well within normal fluctuation. Mentions and cited pages swung erratically rather than trending upward, with one client seeing cited pages jump from 13 to 38 between months one and two, only to fall back to 10 in month three. Which sounds less like a strategy taking hold, and more like ambient noise.

Across both clients, across all four AI platforms we track, we could not identify a single directional signal that pointed to LLMs.txt as a driver of improved visibility. ChatGPT, which represented the largest share of AI mentions in both cases, showed no consistent response to implementation. Neither did Gemini, AI Overviews, or AI Mode.

The file was there but the bots didn't care.

Want to see the full experiment behind this? [Click here](https://www.gomogroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/LLMs.txt_Experiment_Overview.pdf) to explore the methodology, raw data, and what the numbers actually looked like!

## **What's Actually Happening Under the Hood?**

When you look at your server logs after implementing LLMs.txt (as a large number of SEOs and GEO practitioners are now doing) the picture becomes stark. One practitioner managing over 20,000 domains reported in a widely-shared Reddit thread that essentially no major AI bots are downloading or viewing their LLMs.txt files.

This tracks perfectly with what we saw. If no one is reading the file, the file can't help you.

Mueller's comparison to the keywords meta tag extends even further when you think about it mechanically. A bot sophisticated enough to generate useful, cited, contextually accurate responses about your brand has almost certainly already crawled and processed your actual content. At that point, your LLMs.txt is a redundant summary of something it already knows and a potential trust signal it has every reason to treat with skepticism.

## **So Should You Implement It At All?**

Here's our honest, data-backed position:

Based on our testing to date, we do not consider **LLMs.txt to be a meaningful lever for GEO or AI search visibility yet**. Across different clients, industries, and a six-month testing window, we found no measurable evidence of impact. The wider community is arriving at similar conclusions through server log analysis. That said, AI search remains a fast-moving space, and we'll continue reassessing this as platforms evolve and adoption changes.

That said — it won't actively hurt you. Implementation is low-effort, and if the standard does achieve adoption at some future point, you'll be ahead of the curve. But "won't hurt you" is a very different brief from "will help you," and we think it's important to say that clearly.

The hour you spend creating and implementing LLMs.txt is almost always better spent on:

- **Restructuring product and service pages** with clean, extractable, structured data
- **Publishing functional assets** — tools, calculators, original research — that AI systems actually cite
- **Technical SEO hygiene** — fixing crawlability, page speed, and schema markup issues
- **FAQ and entity-rich content** that answers the specific questions AI platforms are surfacing
- **Earning press coverage and authoritative backlinks** that signal trustworthiness to LLM training pipelines

These are the levers that move AI visibility scores. We know because we track them.

## **The Bigger Lesson Here**

The LLMs.txt conversation is a useful mirror for how GEO is maturing as a discipline. Early in any new search channel's evolution, agencies reach for quick wins: for the equivalent of meta keywords, for the early-days PageRank hacks, for whatever the new signal seems to be. And there's nothing wrong with testing these things. Testing is exactly what you should do.

But testing has to mean something. It means instrumenting properly, tracking the right metrics, holding a timeline, and being willing to report what the data says, even when it contradicts the excitement.

At GO MO Group, we ran this experiment because we believe our clients deserve strategies grounded in evidence, not in the content marketing cycles of the tools industry. Based on the evidence available today, we found no measurable impact on AI visibility from implementing LLMs.txt alone. That's what our data shows today, and we'll continue testing and refining our recommendations as the AI search landscape evolves.

AI search is evolving rapidly, and so are our recommendations. As new AI capabilities, standards, and platform behaviours emerge, we'll continue testing approaches like LLMs.txt alongside other GEO initiatives. If the major AI platforms announce genuine adoption (and they may, eventually), we'll revisit this assessment, rerun the experiment, and update our guidance based on the latest evidence.

Until then: build content that AI platforms want to cite. Earn coverage that gives them a reason to trust you. Invest in structured data that makes your pages machine-readable without needing a separate file to explain them.

That's GEO that works. And we have the data to prove it.
